A wedding piano music checklist: ceremony to cocktail hour

Choose the moments that need music before choosing every song. A clear cue sheet, a workable instrument setup and a plan for moving between rooms will help your pianist follow the day as it happens.

Oz Merchavy · Pianist, Bachelor of Music in Piano Performance, Memorial University

This checklist is for couples planning live piano at a wedding, including events in St. John's and elsewhere in Newfoundland. It is a planning aid; confirm your own schedule and equipment arrangements with your musicians and venue.

1. Decide where live music belongs

The ceremony, drinks reception and dinner do different jobs. You can book music for one part of the day or several, but tell the pianist about the gaps as well as the playing time.

  • Guest arrival: background music while people take their seats. Give an approximate start time and agree what happens if the ceremony runs late.
  • Processional: entrance music for the wedding party and couple. Say who is walking, in what order, and whether the music changes between entrances.
  • Signing or an interlude: a flexible selection that can end naturally when the moment is complete. Confirm requirements with the officiant.
  • Recessional: an agreed musical cue after the ceremony, followed by music while guests leave.
  • Cocktail hour or dinner: a longer set with a different energy and volume. Leave space for conversation, announcements and speeches.

A short walk down an aisle rarely needs an entire recording played from beginning to end. Tell your pianist which section matters most: the opening, a melody, or the chorus. They can discuss an arrangement and ending that fit the entrance.

2. Make a cue sheet, not just a playlist

A playlist names the music; a cue sheet tells the musician when to act. Give one person responsibility for cues and make sure the pianist can see them. The example below is a template, not a fixed running time.

Share the final version with the officiant, planner and musicians. If a friend is singing, agree the key, arrangement, rehearsal needs and who starts each piece.

3. Check the piano, power and room changes

A venue having a piano is useful information, but it is not a complete equipment plan. Ask whether it is available for your event, where it sits, and whether it is in playable condition. If tuning or moving is needed, decide who arranges it before the day.

  • Instrument: venue piano or the musician's portable setup?
  • Access: parking, loading route, stairs and time to set up.
  • Space: a stable, dry playing area with room for the chosen ensemble.
  • Power: a suitable supply where an electronic instrument or amplification is required.
  • Sound: guest count, room size, expected conversation level and any venue restrictions.
  • Movement: enough time to relocate equipment between the ceremony and reception.
  • Outdoors: protection from wind and rain, suitable temperature and a ready indoor alternative.
  • Coordination: one venue contact who knows when musicians arrive and where they set up.

For a wedding outside St. John's, include the community and venue address when asking about availability. Travel, access and an overnight stay can affect the plan. Confirm what is included in the quote rather than assuming all events need the same setup.

Oz can bring an instrument when the venue has no suitable piano. Confirm the rest of the equipment and weather arrangements for your particular venue when booking.

4. Choose solo piano or an ensemble

Solo piano offers melody and accompaniment in one instrument and works well for flexible ceremony cues and background sets. Piano and cello or piano and violin adds a distinct melodic voice. A jazz or Latin trio can bring more rhythmic movement to a reception.

Listen to examples that match the part of the wedding you are booking. A recital recording shows musicianship, while a jazz or ensemble performance gives you a different sense of the atmosphere. Consider the room, your preferred repertoire and the space available before deciding on the number of players.

A special request may need an arrangement for the exact lineup. Share the original song or version early and confirm the arrangement, any rehearsal and any additional fee before treating it as booked.

5. Send these details with your enquiry

You do not need a finished playlist to ask about availability. Start with:

  1. Date, community and venue, including whether any part is outdoors.
  2. Which parts of the day need music and the approximate start and finish times.
  3. Guest count, rooms used and any equipment already available.
  4. Solo piano or an ensemble, plus two or three examples of the mood you want.
  5. Must-have songs, a singer or other musician joining, and any timing restrictions.

Ask for written confirmation of the lineup, playing time, breaks, travel, equipment, requested arrangements, payment terms and cancellation terms. These should be agreed for your event, rather than inferred from a sample performance or a directory price range.
